Let's get out our theoretical microscopes, and take a closer look at the [LOP](/sloi/glossary#lop){:target="_blank"}, and see if we can see any logic to 
When it divides evenly, or not.  Going along the LOP, we graph the deficit of each potential factor.  If the deficit is zero, then it's a factor.
<iframe src="/lib/gfx2/?/sloi/widgets/primes/even-gons/floptoids" width="1000" height="600" style="border-style:none"></iframe>

At first glance, it looks fairly random, (I remember thinking it looked like rain drops falling down).  But, after taking a closer look, I realized that there is some definite order, and regular progression from one number to the next.
(Interestingly enough, you can tell by examining a single column that even though it may not be a factor, what the offset to the number where it will be a factor will be). 
Also, it's worth looking at how new columns are added. Looking at the number associated, we see that it happens at x^2, (e.g. 4,9,16...).  

But, if I can wax slightly poetic, there almost appears to be a Cellular Automata like sort of generation thing happening here.  If we consider the progression of numbers as ticks, then looking at the largest column, when it is at a square number, then it will then spawn a predecessor in the next column over the next tick.

This Cellular Automata (CA) like entity, (whimsically called a Floptoid, due to being found on the [FLOP](/sloi/glossary#flop)){:target="_blank"}, will have a single generation where it traverses the length of the column, (grows up), and then the next time it returns to the factor line, it will spawn the next column.  After that, it repeats looping Ad Infinitum.

It turns out that from the last column, here is the formula to calculate where a Floptoid will be:
<pre>
    y = LastColumnX -(col^2))%(2*col)
</pre>

Conceptually we are saying that to find a column position for a given tick, we find the age of each column, based on where it had to be to give birth to the last column, and then account for the motion in ticks between when it gave birth and the current tick.

However, if we take each column in the Floptoid CA, and consider it as an n-gon instead, we get a new model.  Because we also find that because they are all doubled, all the n-gons are all even, hence the "Even-gon" model.

<iframe src="/lib/gfx2/?/sloi/widgets/primes/even-gons/even-gon" width="600" height="500" style="border-style:none"></iframe>

In this model, we see that a Floptoid becomes a factor when it is on the Factorix line.  (Horizontal line in the model).

But, strangely enough, the way the line extends on the right and left, means that a Floptoid can potentially be either a right-handed factor, or left.  

Does this make a difference?  Not really as far as I can tell, (and I guess is a side-effect of the deficit being less than the factor, equal to the factor, and then greater than the factor, before returning to the factor.)  

However, making this explicit does potentially give us more options in the way the model is decomposed.  Specifically, if we "unwrap" the Even-gon model, then we get a wave similar to the following image:
